# Dividend and Low-Volatility Index/Fund Comparison Notes

Use this reference for dividend, high-dividend, dividend-growth, low-volatility, and dividend-low-volatility index products.

## What creates compounding

Long-term investor return is:

> price appreciation + distributions reinvested − fund expenses − taxes − tracking/transaction losses

A high distribution frequency is not itself an advantage. A distribution reduces NAV; compounding depends on underlying earnings/dividend growth and disciplined reinvestment.

## Methodology questions that matter most

- Is yield based on one recent year or a multi-year average?
- Is continuous distribution required?
- Are payout-ratio outliers, negative earnings, leverage, or weak cash flow excluded?
- Is dividend-per-share growth required?
- Is profitability/ROE growth and stability included?
- Is low volatility a final screen or merely a weighting input?
- Is weighting by dividend yield, inverse volatility, market cap, or a composite?
- What are stock and sector caps?
- How often does the index rebalance?

## Common traps

### Dividend trap

A falling share price mechanically raises static yield. Check whether earnings, free cash flow, payout capacity, and dividend per share are deteriorating.

### Cyclical peak yield

Commodity, shipping, financial, and other cyclical companies may distribute heavily at peak earnings. A recent high yield may not survive normalization.

### Hidden industry bet

A portfolio can hold 50–100 securities yet still be a concentrated bet if banks, energy, utilities, property, or SOEs dominate. Examine sector and common-factor exposure, not only constituent count.

### “Low volatility” overclaim

Low volatility is backward-looking. It may reduce ordinary fluctuations without protecting against regime changes, credit events, policy shifts, or industry-wide drawdowns.

### Tax and cross-border leakage

Index factsheets often show pre-tax total return or gross dividend yield. Actual fund/investor return can be reduced by dividend withholding, fund-level taxes, FX, cross-market holidays, settlement, quotas, and tracking losses.

## Index-only quality scoring

Use this mode when the user asks whether a dividend index itself is good, not whether it is cheap now or which ETF to buy.

### Scope boundary

Include only:

1. methodology quality;
2. historical total-return and risk evidence;
3. performance stability;
4. concentration and return-source risk.

Exclude current valuation/yield percentiles (buy timing) and fund fees, scale, liquidity, tracking, distributions, and channels (wrapper quality).

### Default weights

| Dimension | Weight | Suggested subdimensions |
|---|---:|---|
| Methodology | 40% | dividend sustainability 18, dividend-trap defense 8, low-volatility mechanism 6, weighting/rebalance 5, transparency 3 |
| Historical risk-return | 25% | annualized total return 10, volatility 5, maximum drawdown 7, return/volatility 3 |
| Performance stability | 20% | full calendar years 6, rolling 3-year outcomes 6, rolling 5-year outcomes 4, live evidence 4 |
| Structure and return-source risk | 15% | stock concentration 5, sector concentration 6, source interpretability 4 |

Methodology has the highest weight because it determines future holdings; history validates the rule rather than replacing it. Scores are comparison aids, not statistical estimates. Show every dimension and weight whenever publishing totals.

### Historical-evidence requirements

- Use same-currency total-return series with distributions reinvested, never price indices.
- Align dates and calculation conventions across indices.
- Report annualized return, monthly or daily annualized volatility, maximum drawdown, and recovery where available.
- Add full-year hit rate and rolling 3-/5-year outcomes; a single ten-year CAGR can hide unstable paths.
- Separate pre-launch backtests from post-launch live history. Reduce conclusion confidence when backtests dominate rather than pretending simulated and live records are equivalent.
- Do not infer a decade of sector exposure from one current factsheet. If historical constituents/sector weights are unavailable, label the limitation and evaluate only methodology caps plus the current snapshot.
- Do not fabricate a dividend/earnings/valuation return attribution without historical fundamentals. In that case, score whether the methodology and concentration make the likely return source clear and whether one sector can dominate.

### Readable output order

1. one-paragraph conclusion distinguishing the historical winner from the methodology/overall winner;
2. one table mapping each index to its design goal and main trade-off;
3. visually scannable selection chains (`universe → quality screens → yield rank → low-vol screen → weighting/caps`);
4. aligned total-return/risk table;
5. annual and rolling stability table;
6. weighted scorecard with short explanations for surprising ranks;
7. evidence limits and sources.

A useful narrative tension is: “The ten-year winner is not necessarily the best-designed index.” Explain the gap through methodology quality, backtest share, stability, and concentration rather than dismissing the historical result.

## Full investable-product priorities

1. Sustainable methodology and quality filters.
2. Sector/factor concentration.
3. Same-currency, same-tax, same-date total-return evidence.
4. Valuation/yield starting point.
5. Wrapper fees, tracking, liquidity, and purchase channel.

When comparing a dividend-growth methodology with a simple trailing-yield methodology, give structural sustainability meaningful weight. When comparing wrappers of the same index, prefer the lower all-in-cost product once scale, spread, and tracking are demonstrably adequate.

## Useful conditional conclusions

- **Single long-term core:** favor balanced methodology, sustainable distributions, and acceptable concentration over maximum current yield.
- **Current-income or value satellite:** a higher-yield, lower-valuation index can be suitable if tax leakage and payout cyclicality are understood.
- **A-share only:** accept that many dividend-low-volatility indices may carry large bank/financial exposure; quantify it.
- **ETF only:** compare bid/ask spread and actual turnover, not scale alone.
- **OTC recurring investment:** compare share-class purchase/redemption schedules and cash drag; convenience can justify modest extra cost.

## Data-date discipline

Always label separately:

- index return/risk cutoff;
- index holdings and valuation date;
- fund scale reporting date;
- ETF liquidity observation date;
- fee/prospectus version date.

Never merge these into a false single “current” date.
